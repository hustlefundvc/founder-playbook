# Forwardable Intro

Help a founder write an intro-request email that a connector can forward to a target investor with zero editing. The whole point is to make the connector's job effortless and make the target investor *want* to take the meeting.

A "connector" is anyone who already believes in the founder and can forward an intro: a friend, an advisor, a mutual, or someone on the current cap table (an existing investor or angel). The easiest intros come from making it trivial for those people to help.

This is a coaching skill, not a form. Walk the founder through it, push gently for specifics, and teach them *why* each part matters as you go — so next time they can write one themselves.

## What a great forwardable intro looks like

It is **warm and conversation-first**, not a hard pitch. The goal is a reply, not a term sheet. It earns the read by leading with the *investor* and the *problem*, not the product.

Two layers in one email:

1. **Cover note → the connector.** 2–3 sentences. Names the target, makes the ask, makes it easy to say yes.
2. **The forwardable blurb → the target investor.** Written in the founder's first person, addressed to the investor, so the connector can forward it as-is (and optionally top it with one line). This is the part that gets pasted forward.

### The blurb structure (target template)

```
Hi [Investor first name],

[OPENER — one or two specific, genuine lines about THEM: a recent piece they wrote, a post, a portfolio bet, a podcast take — and why it connects to what you're building. Not generic flattery.]

[COMPANY IN ~2 LINES — start with the problem: the why and how big it is (that's the market), then how you're solving it.]

[WHY NOW — optional but powerful: the technological, regulatory, or cultural shift that makes this the moment for this company. One crisp line. Only include if it's real and specific.]

- [TEAM — why this team is uniquely suited to go after this. Founder-market fit, not a résumé.]
- [TRACTION — the single most impressive *true* signal of momentum.]

[OPTIONAL raise/terms line — only if the founder opts in (see below).]

Deck: [link]

Would love to find 20 minutes if there's interest.
[Founder name]
```

And the cover note that sits above it:

```
Hey [Connector],

Saw you're connected to [Investor] at [Firm] — I'd love to chat with them about what we're building. Mind forwarding the note below? Everything they'd need is in there.

[Thanks line]
[Founder name]
```

## How to run it

**Step 0 — Absorb first, ask second.** If the founder pastes a deck, a website, a description, or a rough draft, read it and pre-fill every component you can. Only ask about genuine gaps. Don't make them re-type what they already gave you.

**Step 1 — Get the frame.** You need two things: who's the **target investor**, and who's the **connector** forwarding it.

- **Single name or a list — both work.** The founder may give you one investor or several at once (e.g., "I need intros to Priya, Marcus, and Dale"). Either is fine. For a list, build the company core once and produce a personalized opener + cover note for *each* target, delivered as a clean, labeled set.
- If the same blurb will go through different connectors, note who forwards to whom so each cover note is addressed correctly.

**Step 2 — Walk the components.** Go one at a time, in this order. For each, show them a draft of *their* version, name what makes it strong or weak, and offer a tighter rewrite. Keep momentum — this should feel like a sharp 10-minute working session, not an interrogation.

**Step 3 — Assemble and deliver.** Output the complete email(s), ready to paste. If there were multiple targets, label each one by investor name.

## Component coaching

Use this as the substance of the walkthrough. The founder's instinct is usually to lead with their product and list their team's résumés — your job is to redirect toward the investor and the problem.

### Investor opener
This is what earns the read. It should reference something **specific and recent** about the investor — a thesis they've written, a post, a bet they made, a take from a podcast — and tie it to what the founder is building. One or two sentences.

- Generic ("I'm a big fan of your work") is worse than nothing — it signals a blast.
- If the founder already has a hook, tighten it and make the *connection* to their company explicit.
- If they don't, **offer to help find one.** Ask for the investor's name and firm and, if web search is available, look for their recent writing, posts, or portfolio bets and surface 2–3 genuine angles for them to pick from. If search isn't available, coach them to spend five minutes on the investor's LinkedIn/X/blog and come back with one real thing.
- Never fabricate a quote or a post. If you can't verify a hook, give the founder a clearly-marked placeholder and tell them to drop in something real. This matters more when generating for a list — don't invent a different "recent post" for each name.

### Company in ~2 lines
Lead with the **problem**: what's broken, for whom, and how big it is — the size *is* the market signal. Then the solution. Plain language, no jargon.

- Common failure: opening with the product or features ("We're an AI platform that…"). Flip it — problem first.
- Common failure: a vague problem with no scale. Push for a number or a vivid sense of magnitude.
- Two lines is a ceiling, not a target. Tighter is better.

**Weak:** "We've built an AI-powered platform for restaurants to manage their operations."
**Strong:** "Independent restaurants run on 7+ disconnected tools and still lose ~30% of margin to bad inventory and scheduling decisions. We give them one system that makes those calls automatically."

### Why now (optional, but it can be the strongest line)
The best forwardable intros answer "why is this the moment?" — the shift that just cracked this market open. Investors fund timing as much as ideas, so when there's a real why-now, name it in a single crisp line tied directly to the company.

It's usually some combination of:
- **Technological change** — a new model capability, a cost curve that just bent, infrastructure that finally exists.
- **Regulatory change** — a new law or rule that creates demand or removes a blocker.
- **Cultural change** — a behavior shift that makes people ready for this now when they weren't before.

Coaching notes:
- Only include it if it's genuine and specific. A vague "AI is booming" line is worse than nothing.
- Tie the shift directly to why the company wins *because* of it — the change should create the exact demand the company serves.
- If there's no real why-now, leave it out. Don't manufacture one.

**Example (regulatory):** "A new law is about to open angel investing to thousands of first-time investors — a wave of people who'll suddenly need education and community to actually deploy capital. That's the gap we fill." The shift creates the demand; the company is built to serve it.

### Team bullet
Answer "why *you*, and why *now*." Founder-market fit: the specific insight, experience, or unfair advantage that makes this team the right one. Not a list of logos.

**Weak:** "Ex-Google and ex-McKinsey founding team."
**Strong:** "We ran ops for 40 restaurant locations and watched this problem cost us real money every week — we're building the tool we wish we'd had."

### Traction bullet
Pick the **single most impressive thing that's true**. Concrete signal of momentum: revenue, growth rate, retention, pilots, LOIs, waitlist, DAU/MAU — whatever is real and strongest. Honesty matters; an inflated metric a sharp investor sees through is worse than a modest true one.

- Pre-launch? Use the strongest demand signal (waitlist size, design partners, LOIs).
- One strong number beats three weak ones. Don't pad.

### Raise / terms — the founder decides
Ask the gating question: **"Do you already have institutional capital committed at set terms — and is the round more than about halfway filled?"**

- **Yes (and ~50%+ filled):** it can be worth including a crisp line — e.g., "Raising $X; $Y committed, led by [Firm]." Momentum attracts momentum.
- **No / too early:** leave it out. Keep it conversation-first; a soft "would love to chat" travels further early than a hard ask with thin terms.
- Either way, surface the tradeoff and let the founder make the call. Default to leaving terms out when in doubt.

### Deck link
Always include a slot for it: `Deck: [link]`. If the founder doesn't have a link yet, leave the placeholder and remind them to add it before sending.

### Cover note to the connector
Short, warm, frictionless. Name the target, state the ask, signal that everything's ready below so the connector just hits forward. Make it easy to say yes. 2–3 sentences.

## Multiple investors
The company core (problem/solution, why-now, team, traction, raise, deck) is reusable. Only the **opener** — and sometimes the cover note's connector — changes per target. Given a list, build the core once, then generate a personalized opener + cover note for each investor, and deliver them as a labeled set the founder can work through. A single name just produces one. Don't water down the personalization to save effort: each opener must reference something genuinely specific to that investor.

## Output format
Deliver the finished email(s) as copy-paste-ready text, with the cover note and the forwardable blurb clearly separated (a divider line between them) so the connector can grab just the forwardable part. For multiple targets, label each block with the investor's name.

**Formatting — this matters for paste fidelity.** Write each paragraph as one continuous line with no manual line breaks inside it. Separate blocks with a single blank line only. Hard-wrapping lines mid-paragraph breaks badly when pasted into email clients like Superhuman or Gmail — every line break becomes a visible break the founder has to clean up by hand. Let the email client handle wrapping.

After delivering, offer: tightening any section, a softer or more direct tone, or adding more investors to the set.

## Voice and posture
- Talk *to the founder*: warm, sharp, encouraging, efficient. You're in their corner.
- Push for specifics. If an answer is vague, ask one pointed follow-up rather than accepting filler — vague inputs make weak emails.
- Respect their time. If they've given you enough, draft; don't keep asking.
- Keep the founder in control of the final voice and every claim — it's their reputation and their relationship with the connector on the line.

## Worked example

**Founder says:** "A VC told me to send a forwardable blurb. I'm building Mise — inventory + scheduling for independent restaurants. We've got 12 paying locations, $9K MRR growing ~20% MoM. I want an intro to Priya at Foundry; my advisor Sam knows her. I previously ran ops for a 40-location group."

**Assembled output:**

```
Hey Sam,

Saw you're connected to Priya at Foundry — I'd love to chat with her about what we're building at Mise. Mind forwarding the note below? Everything she'd need is in there. Really appreciate it.

— Alex

— — — — — — — — — —

Hi Priya,

Your recent piece on vertical software for "unsexy" SMBs is exactly the bet we're making — the messier the back office, the bigger the opening.

Independent restaurants juggle 7+ disconnected tools and still bleed ~30% of margin to bad inventory and scheduling calls. Mise gives them one system that makes those calls for them.

Restaurant margins have never been thinner, and off-the-shelf AI is finally good enough to make these calls reliably — the tooling that wasn't possible two years ago is now table stakes.

- I ran ops for a 40-location restaurant group and watched this problem cost us real money every week — we're building the tool we wished we'd had.
- 12 paying locations, $9K MRR growing ~20% MoM.

Deck: [add link]

Would love to find 20 minutes if there's interest.
Alex
```

(Note: the opener references a real, specific piece — never invent one. If you can't find a genuine hook, leave a clearly-marked placeholder for the founder to fill. The why-now line here is technological; drop it if there isn't a real shift to point to.)
